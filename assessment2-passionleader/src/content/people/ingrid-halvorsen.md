Dr. Halvorsen trained as a queueing theorist — a BSc in Mathematics from the
University of Oslo, followed by a PhD at ETH Zürich modelling service-time
variance in unstructured lines — before redirecting a promising quantitative
career toward what she now calls, in grant applications, a "generalisable
verdict-formation methodology": isolate the act, name the contest, state the
verdict. She delivered the method's inaugural keynote to a room she has
since described, in three separate retellings, as "standing."

Her fieldwork catalogues where queues are structurally strong — a single
file along a curb — and where they're structurally ambiguous, like a
cluster of customers several deep at a store counter with no marked line at
all. She maintains what she calls a "formal taxonomy" of cutting behaviour,
running from "excuse me, is this the line" to simply pushing up to the
register, and has resisted, on principle, every suggestion that this is
simply a list.

She also co-teaches Week 9, where the department applies its own method
back onto cases it already built — the elevator, seat-saving, transit
space — a synthesis exercise she markets as "rigorous self-audit" and
everyone else in the department privately calls "checking our own
homework."
