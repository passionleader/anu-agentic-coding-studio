This week studies two cases that share one property: neither is tied to
any single physical space. A queue forms wherever strangers need to agree,
without ever discussing it, on whose turn it is. Litter accumulates
wherever a bin is close enough to reach and somebody chooses not to. Both
are commons behaviors — the pattern repeats at a store counter, a bus stop,
a food stall, or a footpath, with no physical enforcement beyond what
other people are willing to do about it.

A queue is, structurally, nothing: no rope, no numbered ticket in most
cases, just an agreement among strangers about whose turn it is. That it
holds at all — that dozens of people will stand in a line enforced by
nothing but mutual glaring — is this course's cleanest example of a norm
doing the work of a rule, and arguably humanity's most primitive yet
highly organised social protocol: a technology older than writing,
requiring no infrastructure beyond a shared willingness to stand still and
silently resent whoever breaks it. We treat "cutting" as a spectrum: the
person who asks first, the person who exploits an ambiguous merge, and the
person who simply walks to the front, each met with a different intensity
of social response.

Litter's interest is almost the opposite: it has almost no immediate
audience. The person who benefits from not carrying a wrapper to a bin is
rarely watched doing it, and the person who eventually pays is rarely the
person who saw it happen. Isolating the act here means reading the
aftermath instead of watching the moment — the same method as the queue,
applied where there was never really an audience to begin with. This
occurs at the intersection of micro-interactionism and informal
environmental governance, and this course treats it accordingly.

## Learning Objectives

This course's objective is to elevate two minor public irritations —
queue-cutting and a dropped wrapper — to the level of a scholarly
discipline. By the end of this week, students will be able to:

- describe queueing as a civilisational protocol rather than a mere
  convenience, and identify where that protocol is structurally strong
  versus structurally ambiguous
- place a given queue-cutting incident on the cutting spectrum, from
  polite inquiry to unilateral incursion
- classify a discarded item using the Department of Applied Incivility's
  delayed-cost-externality framework, distance from the nearest bin
  included

## Takeaways

Students who complete this week do not simply notice a queue or a dropped
cup — they theorise them. Graduates leave as advanced anthropologists,
qualified to identify a queue-incursion or an externality discharge in
well under a second, and to file both, correctly, as evidence of a
civilisation quietly holding itself together.

## Outline

- the queue as an unenforced norm, and why it holds anywhere strangers form
  a line
- where queues are structurally strong versus structurally ambiguous
- a spectrum of cutting, from "excuse me, is this the line" to walking

- threshold litter: what accumulates before a bin, and what that distance
  tells you about tolerance for inconvenience
- discussion: what queueing and litter share as commons behaviors with no
