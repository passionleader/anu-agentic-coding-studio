A footpath at peak hour behaves like a fluid: a rough average speed, a rough
average following distance, and a system that copes fine with gradual changes
and very badly with sudden ones. Stopping dead to check a phone, read a sign,
or answer a call is one of the few genuinely instantaneous disruptions this
course studies, which makes it the cleanest one to measure.

This week works through the pile-up as a real, describable phenomenon: how
far back the disruption propagates as a function of footpath width and crowd
density, how long it takes to clear, and how many of the people caught in it
ever learn what caused it. Mr. Osei's own research (see his
[profile](/people/emeka-osei/)) is on exactly this question, and the week
leans on it directly. Measuring the pile-up this precisely is what isolating
a single-input disruption looks like when the room can't even see who caused
it.

## Outline

- crowd flow as a fluid-dynamics analogy, and where the analogy breaks down
- the sudden stop as the purest single-input disruption in the course
- measuring the pile-up: distance, duration, and how many people never learn
  why
- case studies: doorway stops, escalator-exit stops, and the phone-check stop

## Learning Objectives

This week's objective is to formalise the sudden pedestrian stop as a
measurable perturbation in an otherwise self-organizing fluid system, and to
elevate a distinctly unremarkable act of thoughtlessness to the status of a
peer-reviewable fluid-dynamics case study. Students should leave able to
name, and disapprove of, a Unilateral Flow Interruption on sight.

## Takeaways

Graduates of this week's material acquire the Department of Ambulatory
Dynamics' signature diagnostic skill: the ability to determine, from the
specific quality of a stutter-step eight bodies back, exactly where and when
the interruption occurred. As far as the department has been able to
determine, this skill has no application whatsoever outside a crowded
footpath.
