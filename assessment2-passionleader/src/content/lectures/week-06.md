The subway carriage or the evening bus puts strangers closer together, for
longer, than almost any other shared space — and gives them almost no tools
for negotiating it beyond posture. This week catalogues the standard moves
of informal spatial requisition: the bag on the empty seat, the manspread,
the pole grip that claims more radius than balance requires, the headphone
volume pitched to be heard rather than merely audible. None of them are
against any rule. All of them cost someone else something. Treat it as an
unpriced commons in the fullest economic sense: there's more space in a
carriage than any timetable accounts for, and no mechanism except posture
and mutual embarrassment decides who gets how much of it. Where Week 3's
elevator fixes everyone's position for the whole ride, transport keeps
recalculating it stop by stop — claimed space here is a moving target,
subject to continuous, unratified renegotiation.

We use this week to also take stock of the course so far — the Midpoint Crit,
in the Drop-in Session, is where you bring your first four case
studies' worth of method and have it stress-tested in front of the room
before you commit to the back half of the course.

## Learning Objectives

This course's objective is to elevate a manspread to the level of a
scholarly discipline. By the end of this week, students will be able to:

- distinguish claimed space from needed space without access to the
  claimant's actual intentions
- classify a given transit posture using the Centre for Transit Ethics'
  NCSR / VSYP framework
- articulate why a moving vehicle's commons resists the fixed-position
  logic that governs Week 3's elevator

## Takeaways

Students who complete this week do not simply survive the evening
commute — they annotate it. Graduates leave equipped to identify a
non-consensual spatial requisition from across a crowded carriage, and to
recognise a voluntary yielding gesture as the small, unthanked act of
infrastructure maintenance it actually is.
