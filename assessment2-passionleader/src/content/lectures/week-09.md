This closes the course's "common manners" thread, and it deliberately
introduces no new physical space. Instead it turns the method itself back
on three cases already built earlier in the course — the elevator (Week 3),
café seat-saving (Week 5), and transit space (Week 6) — and asks, of each,
the same two questions Slop University's Centre for Ambient Ethics has
since formalised, entirely unprompted, as the Isolate–Contest–Verdict (ICV)
Protocol: what makes the act deniable or contestable, and what the Good
manner version actually costs against the harm it replaces.

The point isn't to re-teach these cases. A method that only ever explains
the case it was invented for hasn't really been tested — it's an anecdote
wearing a lab coat. Re-applying it to cases the room already understands
is what shows whether isolating the act, naming the contested part, and
stating a plain verdict actually generalises, or whether giving the same
three steps a three-letter acronym just makes repetition feel like rigour.

## Outline

- why this week revisits rather than introduces: testing the ICV Protocol
  against cases already on the table
- the elevator, revisited: a door-close button that does almost nothing,
  and who gets to decide that's deniable
- café seat-saving, revisited: what changes when a room fills up
- transit space, revisited: intent versus anatomy, one more time
- discussion: where the method held up, and where it flattened something
- your Assignment 2 is due this week — see the
  [assessment page](/assessments/assignment-2/) for what it's marked against

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, students will be able to state the
Isolate–Contest–Verdict Protocol from memory; correctly identify that its
three steps are identical to what Weeks 2 through 8 already did without a
name; and evaluate, without laughing, whether an acronym constitutes a
methodological contribution.

## Takeaways

Graduates of this week will be structurally unable to encounter a door, a
chair, or a seat for the remainder of their lives without running it,
silently, through Isolate–Contest–Verdict. Whether the method has actually
generalised, or whether three cases is simply too small a sample to notice
that it hasn't, is a question the course raises and — in the interest of
intellectual honesty — declines to answer on its own behalf.
