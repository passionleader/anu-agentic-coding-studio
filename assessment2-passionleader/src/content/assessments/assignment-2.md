Design at least three signs or notices — the kind actually posted in a
library, an elevator, a train carriage, a café, or any other public space
this course has studied — each aimed at nudging someone who reads it toward
the more ethical version of a specific behaviour. For each sign, submit the
sign itself (as text, a mockup, or a described layout) and a short written
explanation of the behavioural reasoning behind it.

Each sign must target one specific, named norm violation from the course
(seat-saving, queue-cutting, the sudden stop, and so on) — not a generic
"be considerate" message. The explanation should say why the wording,
framing, or placement you chose is likely to actually change behaviour,
rather than just asserting that it will: who reads it, at what moment, and
what it asks them to notice about their own next action.

## Referencing

Where you draw on nudge theory, behavioural economics, or persuasive-design
literature to justify a sign's approach, cite it properly using a
consistent academic referencing style (APA is the department default).
Plausible, well-reasoned citations to real or real-sounding research
strengthen a design's justification considerably more than an unsupported
claim about "what works."

1000 to 1500 words across the full set, plus the signs themselves.

## What's marked

- **Clarity and specificity of each sign's target behaviour** — is it
  obvious exactly which violation each sign is aimed at, and exactly what
  the "ethical" alternative looks like?
- **Behavioural/persuasive reasoning behind the design** — is the
  explanation grounded in an actual account of how people read and react to
  signage, not just good intentions?
- **Range and originality across the set of signs** — do the signs cover
  meaningfully different norms and approaches, rather than repeating the
  same trick three times?
- **Referencing and presentation quality** — are sources cited consistently,
  and are the signs themselves presented clearly enough to evaluate?
