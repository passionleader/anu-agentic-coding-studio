## Seat-saving as an unrecognised territorial claim

Café seat-saving is, in the strict sense, a territorial dispute: a
unilateral assertion of sovereignty over a table, lodged without treaty,
without a deed, and without so much as a courtesy notification to the
neighbouring powers (other customers), who nonetheless mostly comply. This
phenomenon sits squarely at the intersection of micro-interactionism and
informal property law — a jurisdiction this course insists on taking
seriously specifically because nobody else will.

An unattended jacket enforces a property claim with no deed, no lock, and no
authority behind it but a stranger's willingness to look elsewhere.

- **the claim** — an object left behind, standing in for a person who isn't
  there
- **the enforcement** — nothing but social pressure; anyone could ignore it
  and nothing stops them
- **the variable** — scarcity: how much the claim actually costs someone
  else

> A jacket over a chair, a queue out the door, and a customer already
> holding coffee circling the room for somewhere to sit. Nobody moves the
> jacket. Nobody has to be told why not.

```notes
Ask: has anyone in the room ever contested a saved seat? Almost nobody has —
that's the point.
```

## What breaks the claim

A single occupied seat in an empty café reads as inviolable. The same
jacket, same café, at peak hour — reads completely differently.

This is, in the fullest sense, a border dispute: some hold that saving a
seat for a friend who is minutes away is completely standard, especially
once a room fills up — moving a jacket for a stranger costs nothing.
Others call the same act presumptuous, since it works only because the
claimant is "banking on nobody saying anything" — and read a busy room's
silence as resignation, not agreement. Neither side has ratified a treaty;
both sides act as though one exists.

## Bad manner vs. good manner

🟥 **Bad manner — Unilateral Territorial Annexation (UTA):** At a packed
café with a queue out the door, a customer drapes a jacket over the last
open table and departs to order — a manoeuvre Slop University's Department
of Applied Territoriality classifies as a unilateral annexation, complete
with an implied flag-planting gesture. Under this framework, the claim's
validity is simply asserted rather than negotiated, and enforcement relies
entirely on the surrounding population's reluctance to escalate.

🟩 **Good manner — Provisional Occupancy Protocol (POP):** That same
customer ordering first, then taking the table only once they actually hold
something to sit down with — a claim backed by demonstrated occupancy
rather than declared sovereignty. The Department files this under
"recognised statehood": nobody has to take your word for it, because you
already showed up with the paperwork.
