## From deniable to overt

Not every classroom violation sits in the same place on the spectrum
between "could have been an accident" and "was obviously a choice."

- **the deniable end** — a cough, a sneeze: technically indistinguishable
  from something involuntary, whatever the room privately suspects
- **the overt end** — unwrapping a crinkly snack, taking a phone call:
  nobody can claim either happened by accident
- **what changes in between** — not the cost to the room, necessarily, but
  whether anyone can be held to account for it

This gradient — from the fully deniable to the fully overt — is the
classroom's own contribution to what the field terms the
deniability-accountability interface: the point where micro-interactionism
and informal normative structure negotiate in real time, usually in under
two seconds, without either party noticing.

> A cough lands exactly as a pause opens, and the room can never quite prove
> it was on purpose. A phone rings minutes later, same room, same silence —
> no proof required.

This week's two cases sit at opposite ends of the same spectrum. Ask the
room which one they'd rather be the target of, and why the answer usually
isn't the overt one.

## Case one: the deniable cough

A cough that a room privately reads as deliberate, while remaining
technically indistinguishable from an accident. Three signals decide which
way a room reads it:

1. **Timing** — does it land inside a pause, or on top of someone speaking
2. **Target** — was it aimed anywhere in particular, or genuinely undirected
3. **Aftermath** — was it acknowledged, or did the room go straight back to
   silence

```notes
These three map directly onto the method: timing and target help you
isolate the act, aftermath is where you describe what happens next.
```

## Bad manner vs. good manner: the cough

🟥 **Bad manner — Exhibitionist Viral Projection (EVP):** Some students, on
catching a cold, elect to project the resulting cough directly onto the
facial region of the nearest bystander, apparently as a form of social
display. Slop University's Department of Epidemiological Theatre
classifies this as "deliberate viral diplomacy," and notes a widely-held
(if unexamined) belief among practitioners that social influence scales
with droplet range.

🟩 **Good manner — Responsible Bio-Containment Gesture (RBG):** The
considerate alternative does not broadcast its own virology. Catching the
cough fully inside a sleeve is filed, in Slop University's public-hygiene
framework, under "micro-heroism" — a contribution too small to be thanked
for, and for that reason the only kind of heroism this course actually
endorses.

The gap isn't subtlety: it's whether a cough is treated as involuntary noise
to be contained, or repurposed as a performance for whoever's nearest.

## Case two: overt classroom conduct

Some classroom violations skip deniability entirely. Unwrapping a bag of
crisps, opening a container of strongly-smelling food, or answering a
phone call mid-lecture all involve a visible decision — to bring it, to
open it, to answer it — that nobody watching needs to interpret.

> A phone rings during a lecture. The student answers it, at conversational
> volume, and keeps talking without leaving the room. Nobody needs to
> isolate an ambiguous signal here — the act announces itself.

Where the cough's whole interest is in how hard it is to prove, this case's
interest is the opposite: the act is never in question, so what the room
actually argues about is proportionality — how much disruption a given
choice was worth, and to whom.

## Bad manner vs. good manner: overt conduct

🟥 **Bad manner — Unsolicited Ambient Broadcast (UAB):** A student's phone
rings mid-lecture; rather than treat this as a private failure, they answer
at full conversational volume and keep talking without leaving the room,
while unwrapping a bag of crisps with the other hand — a bundled, two-
channel transmission that Slop University's Department of Epidemiological
Theatre catalogues as "maximal disruption per unit of effort," the crisps
functioning as a low-cost amplifier for a signal the room never
consented to receive.

🟩 **Good manner — Jurisdictional Self-Removal (JSR):** That same student
silencing the phone before class and, if a call still needs answering,
stepping into the corridor to take it — voluntarily relocating the
disruption outside the room's jurisdiction rather than exercising it
inside. Filed, like the sleeve-catch above, under micro-heroism: nobody
claps, and that is precisely the point.

## Discussion

The cough asks the room to prove intent it can never quite prove. The
phone call asks nothing of the sort — intent was never in doubt. What
should "case study" mean when one violation's whole interest is deniability
and the other's is that there was never anything to deny? Some in the field
have proposed that both cases are, at bottom, instances of the same
territorial claim — a classroom being nothing more than a room in which
several people have temporarily and non-consensually agreed to share a
jurisdiction over silence.
