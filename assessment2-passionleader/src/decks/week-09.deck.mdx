# Towards a Unified Theory of Deniable Discourtesy

## SLOP2950 · Week 9 · A Meta-Analytic Synthesis

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> No door has ever started a war. No jacket has ever collapsed an economy.
> No pair of outstretched legs has ever ended a dynasty. This week asks why,
> in that case, it always feels like they might.

---

## What this week is, and isn't

This is the closing beat of the "common manners" thread, and it introduces
no new physical space. Instead, it re-applies this course's core method —
what Slop University's Centre for Ambient Ethics has now, without being
asked, formalised as the **Isolate–Contest–Verdict (ICV) Protocol** — to
three cases already on the table:

- **the elevator** (Week 3) — a closed system, contested door-holding
- **café seat-saving** (Week 5) — an unenforced claim, contested by scarcity
- **transit space** (Week 6) — an unpriced commons, contested between intent
  and anatomy

```notes
The point isn't to re-teach these cases. It's to ask what the method looks
like once you already know the answer — does isolating the act still add
anything, or does giving it a three-letter name just make repetition feel
like rigour?
```

---

## Naming what Dr. Halvorsen already does

Dr. Halvorsen's own working notes for this week refer throughout to "the
usual three steps." The Centre for Ambient Ethics has since formalised
this, unprompted and to no request from any accrediting body, as the
Isolate–Contest–Verdict (ICV) Protocol:

1. **Isolate** — separate the act from the excuse surrounding it (running
   late, having a cold, this being *your* seat too)
2. **Contest** — name the exact thing a bystander could plausibly argue
   about out loud without sounding unreasonable
3. **Verdict** — state which version costs less, and to whom

This is, verbatim, what Weeks 2 through 8 already did, one case at a time,
without a name. The Protocol's sole original contribution is the acronym.

---

## The same three questions, three times over

For each of the three cases, this week asks the same pair of questions the
ICV Protocol has used all along:

1. **What makes the act deniable or contestable** — the thing that lets
   someone argue it wasn't really a violation at all
2. **What does the Good manner version actually cost** — and is that cost
   smaller than the one it replaces

> Door-holding costs a few seconds shared by everyone already inside.
> Ordering before claiming a café table costs nothing but the assumption
> that the seat was already yours. Pulling your legs in on a crowded bus
> costs exactly the space you were never using.

## Revisiting the elevator

Fixed capacity, fixed exits, short duration, high density — Week 3's
Institute of Vertical Micro-Politics still classifies the car as the
smallest fully sovereign territory a stranger can involuntarily be
annexed into, and nothing about revisiting the case changes the verdict.

🟥 **Bad manner — Unilateral Border Closure (UBC), reprised from Week 3:**
Holding the close-door button on someone jogging toward the car, on the
reasoning that they should have moved faster — a unilateral act of
territorial enforcement that costs the enforcer nothing and the excluded
party a full diplomatic delay.

🟩 **Good manner — Open-Border Transit Accommodation (OBTA), reprised from
Week 3:** Holding the door the few extra seconds it takes them to arrive —
a cost every passenger already inside shares for a moment, against a cost
the latecomer would otherwise carry alone.

## Revisiting seat-saving

An unattended jacket enforces a claim with no deed, no lock, and no
authority behind it but a stranger's willingness to look elsewhere — Week
5's Department of Applied Territoriality's reading of the case holds
exactly as well the second time through.

🟥 **Bad manner — Unilateral Territorial Annexation (UTA), reprised from
Week 5:** Draping a jacket over the last open table at a packed café and
departing to order — a claim asserted rather than negotiated, enforced
entirely by the surrounding population's reluctance to escalate.

🟩 **Good manner — Provisional Occupancy Protocol (POP), reprised from
Week 5:** Ordering first, then taking the table only once there's actually
something to sit down with — a claim backed by demonstrated occupancy
rather than declared sovereignty.

## Revisiting transit space

None of the standard moves — the bag on the empty seat, the wide pole grip,
the pitched-up headphones — break a written rule. Week 6's Centre for
Transit Ethics still reads the carriage as a commons with no pricing
mechanism except posture, and posture hasn't changed since.

🟥 **Bad manner — Non-Consensual Spatial Requisition (NCSR), reprised from
Week 6:** Stretching both legs across an empty seat and keeping a bag on
the seat rather than the lap, while passengers standing in the aisle hold
the rail and say nothing — a requisition the surrounding population
absorbs in silence, which is not the same thing as consent.

🟩 **Good manner — Voluntary Spatial Yielding Protocol (VSYP), reprised
from Week 6:** Pulling the legs in and moving the bag to the lap or the
floor the moment the carriage starts to fill — a unilateral de-annexation,
completed before anyone has to file a claim.

## Does the method generalise, or does it just multiply?

Every case this course has catalogued — the cough, the elevator button,
the café jacket, the bus seat, the queue, the phone call — is, on its own
terms, a matter no reasonable civilisation should ever have needed a
taxonomy for. Nobody's empire has fallen to a badly-held door. And yet,
across three entirely unrelated physical systems — a box, a chair, a seat —
the same two-step verdict format keeps landing in the same place, which is
either evidence that the ICV Protocol has found something real, or
evidence that any method looks like it has found something real once
you've built three test cases specifically to fit it.

Slop University's Centre for Ambient Ethics declines, on the record, to
adjudicate which. Its official position, filed at the request of no one,
is that the distinction is "a problem for a different, better-funded
department."

```notes
This is the discussion to actually have live: where the ICV Protocol held
up cleanly across all three cases, and where forcing a case into the Bad
manner / Good manner shape flattened something that mattered. Push back on
students who say "it always works" — ask them what the method would look
like failing, and whether they'd notice.
```
