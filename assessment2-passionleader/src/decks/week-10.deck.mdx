## A fragile, high-effort norm

The public restroom's silence pretence is arguably one of the most elaborate
voluntary information-suppression regimes ever sustained by a species with
no formal government, no written charter, and no signatories. Everyone
present agrees, without ever saying so, to act as though the room is silent
— a norm with no enforcement beyond how much effort people are willing to
keep putting into it.

- **the mask** — taps run to cover sound both parties already know isn't
  actually covered
- **the drop** — conversations that would be casual anywhere else fall to
  near nothing
- **the silence about the silence** — nobody acknowledges any of it; the
  system depends on that

> A call continues on speakerphone in a stall, no tap running. In the
> stalls and at the sinks, other people don't say anything directly — one
> starts flushing repeatedly instead, until the call stops.

Confrontation is rare here for a specific reason: naming the breach out
loud means admitting you could hear it, which breaks the room's pretence as
completely as the phone call did — so the room's own countermeasures (the
repeated flush, the pointed cough) stay indirect too.

## Bad manner vs. good manner

🟥 **Bad manner — Unmasked Acoustic Disclosure (UAD):** Some patrons, mid-call,
decline to run any masking tap and maintain full conversational volume
inside a stall — loud enough that everyone at the sinks catches both the
topic and the name being discussed. The Slop University Acoustic Ethics Lab
classifies this as "unmasked acoustic disclosure," and has observed that the
offending party is typically the last person in the room to realise how
much has just been disclosed.

🟩 **Good manner — Ritual Tap Deployment (RTD):** The same call, continued
only once the tap is engaged and the voice dropped to near a whisper. The
Lab files this under "ritual tap deployment" — a gesture it is careful to
note fools absolutely no one, and whose entire value lies in being performed
anyway.
