# Common Manners III: Applying the Framework

## SLOP2950 · Week 9

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> The test of a method isn't whether it explains the case you built it on —
> it's whether it still works on the next one.

---

## What this week is, and isn't

This is the closing beat of the "common manners" thread, and it introduces
no new physical space. Instead, it re-applies this course's core method —
isolate the act, name what makes it deniable or contested, state the
verdict as a Bad manner / Good manner pair — to three cases already on the
table:

- **the elevator** (Week 3) — a closed system, contested door-holding
- **café seat-saving** (Week 5) — an unenforced claim, contested by scarcity
- **transit space** (Week 6) — an unpriced commons, contested between intent
  and anatomy

```notes
The point isn't to re-teach these cases. It's to ask what the method looks
like once you already know the answer — does isolating the act still add
anything?
```

---

## The same three questions, three times over

For each of the three cases, this week asks the same pair of questions the
method has used all along:

1. **What makes the act deniable or contestable** — the thing that lets
   someone argue it wasn't really a violation at all
2. **What does the Good manner version actually cost** — and is that cost
   smaller than the one it replaces

> Door-holding costs a few seconds shared by everyone already inside.
> Ordering before claiming a café table costs nothing but the assumption
> that the seat was already yours. Pulling your legs in on a crowded bus
> costs exactly the space you were never using.

## Revisiting the elevator

Fixed capacity, fixed exits, short duration, high density — and a
door-close button that does almost nothing in most buildings, which is
exactly what makes holding it against a latecomer so easy to justify and so
hard to prove was deliberate.

**Bad manner:** Holding the close-door button on someone jogging toward the
car, on the reasoning that they should have moved faster.

**Good manner:** Holding the door the few extra seconds it takes them to
arrive — a shared cost against a cost the latecomer would otherwise carry
alone.

## Revisiting seat-saving

An unattended jacket enforces a claim with no deed, no lock, and no
authority behind it but a stranger's willingness to look elsewhere — and
whether that claim holds turns entirely on how scarce seats are at the
moment someone tests it.

**Bad manner:** Draping a jacket over the last open table at a packed café
and going to order, holding it for the ten minutes the line takes to move.

**Good manner:** Ordering first, then taking the table only once there's
actually something to sit down with.

## Revisiting transit space

None of the standard moves — the bag on the empty seat, the wide pole grip,
the pitched-up headphones — break a written rule. All of them cost another
passenger something, in a carriage with no mechanism except posture to
decide who gets how much of it.

**Bad manner:** Stretching both legs across an empty seat and keeping a bag
on the lap while three people stand holding the rail.

**Good manner:** Pulling in and freeing the seat the moment the carriage
starts to fill, before anyone has to ask.

![Bad manner: feet up across the seats on a bus, occupying more than one
seat's worth of space at once.](./assets/week-06/bad-bundled-verdict.jpg)

## Does the method generalise?

Three cases, three different physical spaces, the same two questions
answered each time. Where the method held up cleanly, and where forcing a
case into the Bad manner / Good manner shape flattened something that
mattered — that's this week's actual discussion.
