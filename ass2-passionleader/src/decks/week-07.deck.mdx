## Two commons, one property

Neither of this week's cases belongs to a single physical space. A queue
forms wherever strangers need to agree, without discussing it, on whose
turn it is; litter accumulates wherever a bin is close enough to reach and
somebody chooses not to.

- **no fixed venue** — a store counter, a bus stop, a food stall, a
  footpath — the pattern repeats wherever the commons does
- **no physical enforcement** — nothing stops a cut, and nothing stops a
  dropped wrapper, except what other people are willing to do about it
- **the shared question** — how much does it cost to do the small, costless
  thing instead

---

## Where the agreement holds, and where it doesn't

- **structurally strong** — a single-file line with one clear front; almost
  never contested
- **structurally ambiguous** — a cluster near a counter, or a fork that
  never cleanly re-merged
- **the counter case** — one line feeding several service points, moving at
  several times the speed of any single lane

> A store counter with no marked lane at all, and a customer who arrived
> last steps straight up to the register the moment it frees — not into an
> empty line, but ahead of several people who were already standing there.

The least contested version of this case: where a single counter serves
multiple staff, the settled answer is one line moving at multiple speeds,
not several separate lines — precisely the setup this week's bad-manner
case exploits by treating an unmarked counter as a free-for-all instead.

---

## The cutting spectrum

From "excuse me, is this the line?" through an exploited ambiguous merge, to
simply walking to the front — each met with a different intensity of
reaction, and none of it specific to any one venue: the same spectrum shows
up at a store counter, a bus stop, a food stall, or a public restroom queue.

```notes
Ask the room which end of the spectrum actually makes them angriest. It's
rarely the most extreme one.
```

## Bad manner vs. good manner: queueing

**Bad manner:** At a busy counter with no marked line, a customer who
arrived after everyone else pushes straight up the moment it's free, ahead
of people who were already standing there waiting their turn.

**Good manner:** That same customer checking who else is waiting and
asking, "who's next?" before stepping up — letting the line's own unmarked
order settle the question instead of just claiming it.

## Threshold litter: the commons' other unpriced cost

What accumulates in the last few metres before a bin — and what that
distance actually tells you about tolerance for inconvenience. Littering
has almost no immediate audience: the person who benefits from not
carrying a wrapper to a bin is rarely watched doing it, and the person who
eventually pays is rarely the person who saw it happen.

> Empty packaging on the pavement, a public bin plainly visible a few
> steps away — close enough that reaching it would have cost less effort
> than the drop itself.

Not every case is this clean. Whether smaller residue — a piece of chewing
gum, a cigarette butt — counts as "the same kind of littering" as dropped
packaging is a live disagreement; and sometimes the missing bin, not the
person, is the actual constraint — several city transit systems removed
public bins outright for unrelated security reasons, which reshuffles who
the delayed cost lands on.

## Bad manner vs. good manner: the litter

**Bad manner:** Walking past a public bin, close enough to reach it without
breaking stride, and letting an empty drink cup fall to the ground instead —
leaving it for whoever cleans up, or nobody.

**Good manner:** Carrying that same cup the extra few steps to the bin and
setting it inside — a smaller effort than the one just avoided.

## Discussion

Both cases share a structural gap: no witness at the moment that matters,
and no fixed venue that pins either one to one physical space. What should
"case study" mean when a norm's whole shape depends on strangers who never
discuss it, wherever they happen to be standing?
