How this simulator actually works

Every number and equation below is exactly what js/weather-simulation.js and js/atmosphere-grid.js compute each frame — nothing on this page is aspirational.

Two coupled fields, not one

The map sits on a grid, same as the convection simulator this project started from — but instead of a single incompressible fluid, two fields evolve together every step: temperature T (°C) and atmospheric pressure P (hPa). Neither one is a passenger: pressure differences push the wind, temperature differences push the pressure, and the wind then carries both fields around. Each step does the following, in this exact order:

  1. Diagnose a target pressure from the current temperature field and any pressure sources (see below), then relax the real pressure field toward it, diffuse it, and advect it by the existing wind.
  2. Turn the pressure gradient into wind. The force pushing air from high to low pressure is deflected by Coriolis rotation and slowed by surface friction, solved together in one unconditionally stable step (see below).
  3. Diffuse and advect the wind itself, the same semi-Lagrangian technique the convection simulator uses for velocity.
  4. Inject heat/cold from any active source, diffuse and advect temperature by the wind it just helped produce, then relax it toward the ambient latitude gradient.

There's no incompressibility projection here — pressure isn't a Lagrange multiplier enforcing zero divergence, it's a real forced, diffusing, advecting field in its own right. That's the central way this solver differs from the convection simulator's.

Why pressure falls where it's warm

Every cell's "target" pressure is a baseline that falls where temperature is above a fixed reference and rises where it's below it, plus the footprint of any user-placed High/Low sources:

Ptarget = 1013.25 − Ktemp · (T − Tref)

Ktemp is the "Thermal-Pressure Coupling" slider, and Tref is fixed at 0°C — the midpoint between the equator and pole temperatures the map is built around. It has to stay fixed rather than tracking the local latitude's own ambient temperature: if it tracked ambient, the anomaly (T − Tref) would sit at exactly zero everywhere once temperature settles, since temperature relaxes toward that same ambient value — no pressure gradient could ever form from the equator-pole gradient alone. Pinning Tref instead means the equilibrium pressure genuinely varies with latitude, a real "thermal low/high" effect, which combined with Coriolis is what drives persistent wind even on an empty map before you place a single source.

Coriolis: why wind spirals, and which way

The Coriolis parameter uses Earth's real rotation rate, not a tuned stand-in:

f = 2 · Ω · sin(latitude)

Ω ≈ 7.2921 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s is Earth's actual sidereal rotation rate — the status bar's "f" readout is this exact formula, live, in the same ×10⁻⁴/s units textbooks use. It's positive in the Northern Hemisphere and negative in the Southern, which is the entire reason flow rotates opposite ways on each side of the Latitude slider's zero point, and vanishes (no rotation at all) right at the equator — the Equator preset exists specifically to show that. Wind never flows straight from high to low pressure here: the pressure-gradient force gets deflected sideways by this term until it settles into a near-balance, the same geostrophic balance that makes real isobars on a weather chart a map of the wind, not just of the pressure.

An integrator that can't blow up

The Simulation Speed slider can push a single step to represent hours of real time at once. A naive step-by-step (explicit Euler) update of Coriolis and friction together is only stable below a timestep that shrinks as friction drops and latitude rises — exactly the corner a low-Friction, high-Latitude, high-Speed combination reaches. Rather than cap the sliders to dodge that, the wind update solves Coriolis and friction together with the trapezoidal (Crank–Nicolson) rule, which stays stable and well-behaved no matter how large a step it's given. At zero friction it's also exactly energy-conserving for the rotation itself, rather than quietly bleeding speed away the way a simpler always-stable scheme would.

Reading the map

  • The heatmap is the solved temperature grid itself, drawn straight from the solver's array (blue colder than 0°C, white at 0°C, red hotter).
  • Isobars and the H/L markers are traced and detected directly from the live pressure grid — the lines are a marching-squares contour trace at fixed 4 hPa intervals, and each H/L is a local extremum found by comparing every cell against its neighbours.
  • Clouds are a pure visual reading of the pressure field, not a separately simulated quantity: coverage rises the further local pressure sits below baseline, the same real tendency for lows to be the cloudy, unsettled systems and highs to be the clear ones.
  • The wind is thousands of tiny particles, each carrying no physics of its own — exactly like the convection simulator's cosmetic tracers, every one is just moved each frame by sampling the solver's real velocity field at its current position. What makes them read as flowing streamlines rather than a field of drifting dots is a separate trail buffer that's never fully cleared, only faded a little every frame, so a particle's own recent past positions persist as a fading streak; each fades in on spawn and out before it dies or drifts off the map, and moves faster and draws longer wherever the underlying wind is stronger.
  • The coastline and its inland contour lines all trace the same static, procedurally generated height field — ridged noise tuned for a jagged, high-frequency topographic look rather than the smooth rounded blob a simpler noise function would give. Only the coastline itself isn't purely decorative the way the inland contours are: cells above sea level genuinely relax temperature faster and add extra surface friction to the wind, a toy stand-in for how real land and sea behave differently, and it's this same land/sea split (thresholded from that height field) that feeds that physics.
  • The graticule is a mix of real and decorative: its horizontal lines are genuine latitude (the same coordinate the temperature gradient and Coriolis both use), but its vertical lines carry no meaning — this is a flat regional slice, not a globe with real longitude wraparound.
