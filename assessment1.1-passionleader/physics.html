How this simulator actually works

Every number and equation below is exactly what js/simulation.js and js/fluid-grid.js compute each frame — nothing on this page is aspirational.

A real fluid solver, not a particle toy

Air in the simulator lives on a grid — a 2D array of cells, each holding a velocity (u, v) and a temperature T. Every frame, the solver advances that grid through the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations using Jos Stam's "Stable Fluids" method (1999): a semi-Lagrangian scheme that stays numerically stable no matter how large a timestep or how fast the flow gets — the property that makes an interactive, real-time fluid sim possible at all. Each step does five things, in this exact order:

  1. Add forces. Buoyancy pushes warmer-than-ambient cells up and colder-than-ambient cells down (see below).
  2. Diffuse velocity. Viscosity spreads momentum into neighboring cells, solved implicitly via Jacobi iteration — unconditionally stable, unlike an explicit diffusion step.
  3. Project. Real air is incompressible: it can't pile up or empty out of a cell. This step solves a pressure field and subtracts its gradient from the velocity until the flow is divergence-free.
  4. Advect. Each cell's velocity gets carried along by the flow itself — traced backward in time to find where its value came from, then bilinearly sampled from there.
  5. Diffuse and advect temperature the same way, as a passive scalar riding along with the now-solved velocity field, then inject heat near any active source.

Walls are just grid cells marked solid: their velocity is pinned to zero (no-slip) and they're excluded from every neighbor's diffusion/pressure stencil, so a wall reads as "no flow through here" without any special-case collision code.

Why hot air rises: the Boussinesq approximation

Real air is compressible — its density genuinely drops as it warms. Solving that in full is far more expensive than a real-time budget allows, so (like almost every convection simulation, real engineering ones included) this one uses the Boussinesq approximation: density is treated as constant everywhere it doesn't matter, and its only effect is a buoyant force proportional to how far a parcel's temperature has drifted from ambient:

abuoyancy = g · β · (T − Tambient)

g is gravity (9.81 m/s²) and β is air's thermal expansion coefficient, β = 1 / Tambient in kelvin — recomputed every call from whatever the ambient temperature slider is currently set to, not a hardcoded constant, since it genuinely depends on the absolute temperature of the room.

Real air's numbers, and why the sim doesn't use them directly

The simulator's constants for air are the real, textbook ones at room temperature:

  • Kinematic viscosity ν ≈ 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s
  • Thermal diffusivity α ≈ 2.1 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s
  • Prandtl number Pr = ν / α ≈ 0.71 — the ratio of how fast momentum diffuses versus how fast heat diffuses, a fixed material property of air

Plugged into the Rayleigh number below at room scale, real air comes out deep in the turbulent regime — Ra in the hundreds of millions. A grid coarse enough to run in a browser at 60fps has nowhere near the resolution to actually capture that turbulence; it would just go numerically unstable and blow up. So the solver's own diffusion instead uses an eddy viscosity: both ν and α are scaled up by the same "Eddy Viscosity ×" slider, which boosts the magnitude of diffusion for numerical stability while keeping their ratio — the effective Prandtl number — exactly what real air's is. This is the same move real-time and large-eddy turbulence models make: model the large, visible structures faithfully, and let a single tuned viscosity stand in for the cascade of small eddies too fine to resolve.

The Rayleigh number: will it even convect?

The status bar's "Ra" reading is the dimensionless number that governs whether buoyancy actually wins against viscous and thermal dissipation — the ratio of the force driving convection to the forces resisting it:

Ra = g · β · ΔT · L³ / (ν · α)

where ΔT is the temperature difference driving the flow and L is a characteristic length (the room's height, here). It's computed against real, unscaled air — answering "if this were a real room, would it actually convect" — regardless of the eddy viscosity the solver itself is running with. Three regimes:

  • Ra < 1700 — conduction-dominated: below the classic critical Rayleigh number (≈1708) for the onset of Rayleigh–Bénard convection in a fluid layer heated from below. Heat just conducts through; buoyancy isn't strong enough to organize a flow.
  • 1700 ≤ Ra < 10⁶ — steady convection cells: buoyancy wins, and the result is smooth, orderly rolling currents — what the convection-cell preset is built to show.
  • Ra ≥ 10⁶ — turbulent convection: past a representative (not exact — the real threshold depends on geometry) boundary where the flow stops being orderly. Real room-scale air, as noted above, lives here.

Reading the canvas

  • The color field is the solved temperature grid itself, drawn straight from the solver's own array (blue colder than ambient, white at ambient, red hotter) — not a separate visualization layer.
  • The streaks are cosmetic tracers with no physics of their own: each one is just a point that gets moved every frame by sampling the solver's real, solved velocity field at its current position — literally the same field the physics runs on. Their length and brightness scale with local speed, like a wind map.
  • Glowing markers are heat/cold sources — continuous forcing that relaxes nearby grid cells toward the source's temperature every frame, not a one-shot injection, so it keeps driving convection as long as it's there.
